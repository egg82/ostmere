# Creature design: principles for Ashfall

General principles from existing works. No Ashfall creatures here.

**Source quality:** the web yielded little first-hand designer testimony. Most
material is fan analysis, trade press and blogs. Items marked **(P)** are from
pages opened and read; **(S)** are from search snippets only; **(K)** are
background knowledge, unverified here. Where a sub-question came back empty, that
is stated.

## 1. Readability

A creature must survive being drawn small and fast.

- Silhouette is the primary test. A design should read in solid black, in grayscale,
  at distance and in motion. Test it at blockout, because detail added later does not
  fix weak primary shapes (P: Rocketbrush).
- Shape language (round = soft, square = heavy and stable, triangle = threat) is a
  convention readers parse before detail. Treat it as a default to *play against
  deliberately*, not a law (P: Rocketbrush).
- If a design reads only through colour, its shapes are too weak. A journal sketch is
  ink, so this is the right test for Ashfall (P: Rocketbrush).
- The set should contrast with itself: "cast contrast matters as much as individual
  design" (S: studio blogs). The "70% of impact is silhouette" figure has no
  primary source (S).
- Scale cues: the Rocketbrush page says little. Established field-sketch practice is
  to put a known object beside the animal (K); a hand, a boot, a tool.
- Not found: any Hollow Knight or Darkest Dungeon designer statement on silhouette
  (searched; null).

Rules:
1. One sentence of silhouette per creature ("a tall thing with too many elbows").
   If it cannot be said, it cannot be sketched.
2. Do the black-fill test before adding side studies.
3. Give every creature one scale reference in the sketch.
4. Vary silhouettes across the bestiary so no two share an outline.

## 2. Uncanny and eldritch

Evidence is thinner than the folklore suggests. What the sources do support:

- **Familiar with wrong details.** Annihilation's reviews describe flowering antlers
  on deer and hybrid animals: recognisable species with an off detail, set against
  real beauty (S: Middlebury, Gizmodo AU snippets). The VFX team grounded the
  mutations in real biology (tumour and cancer references) and aimed for "fantastical
  yet recognizable", with creatures behaving in ways an audience accepts
  (P: VFX Voice).
- **Sorrow over disgust.** Miyazaki rejected a maggot-covered dragon concept and asked
  for "the deep sorrow of a magnificent beast"; enemies are shown as pitiable, still
  fighting for their lives. Lore is carried by which enemies appear and how they
  look, not item text (P: Kill Screen).
- **Detail and the human body.** Junji Ito's uncanny effect is credited to dense,
  detailed drawing of twisted human forms and to a single-page reveal after
  panel-by-panel buildup; also to stretching a mundane anxiety (P: Horror Obsessive;
  fan analysis).
- **Restraint.** SCP's clinical register works by negative space and by not
  speculating on what the reader can work out (P: SCP wiki essay; S: Kris Yotam
  guide). SCP's advice for repetition: repeated behaviour implies limited agency or
  single-mindedness, so give it a reason (P).
- **Movement matters.** A study of virtual animals found uncanny-valley effects for
  near-real animals, stronger for morbid images, and stylised animals avoid it
  (S: Tilburg, Discover).
- Not found: designer sources for Slay the Princess, Darkest Dungeon's bestiary,
  or the Witcher's bestiary on "wrongness" (searched; null). Do not cite them for
  this topic.

Rules:
1. Start from a real animal and change one thing the eye finds *after* it has
   accepted the animal: a count, a joint, a human trait in the wrong place.
2. Prefer wrong behaviour to wrong looks. A normal-looking animal doing one thing an
   animal would not is stronger than a bizarre-looking one.
3. Pair wrongness with beauty or pathos; disgust alone is the cheap version.
4. Give the note-writer a reason to *not* explain. Record what was seen; leave the
   cause out. Dry humour comes from the clinical register meeting the wrong fact.
5. Keep the eldritch to a few, and keep each unexplained at the same depth.

## 3. Speculative biology and anatomy as the design driver

This is the section to apply hardest. It is the same rule as the game's "follow the
premise's consequences honestly."

Method (the questions to answer before drawing):
How does it eat, and what eats it? How does it breed? Where did it come from
(evolved from what, or made by whom, for what)? What does the broken premise change
about the above? Then let the body follow.

- **Dixon, After Man.** Survey the biomes and the adaptations that live in them, then
  design new animals descended from existing ones that share those adaptations.
  Each gets a binomial name and text on behaviour and interactions. Animals fill
  niches: rabbit-descendants take the deer niche; rats become top land predators.
  The setting premise (survivors of a mass extinction) explains the whole fauna
  (P: Wikipedia After Man).
- **Naish on speculative zoology** (P: Sci Am interview). Respect ancestral anatomy:
  starting body plans constrain what can evolve. Ask whether there is a selective
  reason. Test mechanical feasibility (can that structure actually uncouple?). The key
  contribution of Dixon was building whole faunas, not isolated animals.
- **Energetics.** An ASU world-builder guide recommends estimating a large animal's
  food need from predator-prey biomass ratios; an apex predator implies the
  infrastructure that feeds it. Cave animals lose pigment and eyes to save energy.
  Life-history trade-offs (fast breeders against slow growers) shape temperament
  (P: ASU News, describing a guidebook that was not read itself).
- **All Tomorrows** (P: Wikipedia): bodies come from who held power over the species.
  A designed form reflects its maker's purpose, an evolved form reflects local
  pressures. For a world broken by *made* magic, both origins are available, and
  tell them apart on the page.
- **Annihilation**: the Shimmer's rule (refraction of DNA) is applied to
  whatever is inside, so creatures are consequences of one rule rather than a
  zoo (P: VFX Voice for the biology-grounded approach; S: reviews for the
  rule's reach).
- Not opened: Alien Biospheres, Nevernight, and Terryl Whitlatch's own writing. The
  Whitlatch Wikipedia page confirms a science-trained, anatomy-teaching designer but
  states no principles, so any "anatomy-first" claim about her here is **(K)**, not
  verified. Nothing on Nevernight was searched or read.

Rules:
1. Write the answer to eat / be eaten / breed / origin / premise-effect *first*; draw
   second. A feature with no answer to "what is it for" is a candidate cut or a
   deliberate mystery.
2. Start from a real body plan and bend it by one consequence of the premise.
   Constraints from the ancestor will show: use them for credible leftovers.
3. Check the energy budget: what does it eat, how much, and is there that much?
4. Decide origin per creature: evolved under the new rule, or made. Mark which.
5. Give every premise-driven change a second-order effect (on prey, on habitat, on
   the people who live nearby).

## 4. Ecology and a coherent set

- Monster Hunter: World's director calls a solid ecosystem with the right food chain
  the first step in designing monsters. Each area has an herbivore, a predator that
  hunts it and a stronger one above; monsters treat others as food when hungry; the
  map was redesigned to fit the ecosystem. Wilds adds humans into the ecology
  (P: Inven Global lecture write-up; S: Press Start).
- Sets cohere through families, each with a distinct problem: in Windrose, wildlife
  teaches spacing, pirates target priority, undead attrition (S: fan wiki; weak
  source).
- Witcher: monsters are rooted in European folklore, with contracts weaving monster
  into a human story. The "ecology" angle is not sourced (S; null on ecosystem).
- The same premise-driven rule from section 3 is what stops a bestiary becoming a zoo.

Rules:
1. Draw the food web before the bestiary: who eats whom, who is the apex, who is
   the scavenger.
2. Every creature's presence is explained by its place (diet, habitat, premise
   effect) and its absence elsewhere is explained too.
3. Group in families sharing a cause, so the player can learn a rule and apply it.
4. Tie creatures to human consequences: a damaged crop, a closed road, a tale.

## 5. Mundane versus monstrous; escalation

Evidence is thin; this section is mostly inference from the sources above.

- Monster Hunter escalates by geography: weak monsters on outer plains, strong ones
  deeper inland (P).
- Windrose moves wildlife, then human enemies, then undead and plague by biome, and
  keeps supernatural threats separate from mundane ones (S: fan wiki).
- Annihilation's most affecting animals are ordinary ones changed (P/S).
- Not found: a design article on avoiding power creep (searched; null).

Rules (inference, unverified):
1. Make ordinary animals dangerous through the premise (changed habits, disease,
   hunger) so mundane and monstrous share one logic.
2. Escalate by *kind* and region, not size: the next region asks a different
   question of the survivor.
3. Keep true monsters few so they stay events; the mundane tier carries the
   survival economy.
4. Do not escalate with bigger numbers on the same creature; introduce a new rule.

## 6. Naturalists' field notes

- Grinnell system: field notebook (quick, in the moment), field journal (full
  sentences written the same day, ideally within 24 hours), species account
  (detailed behaviour per species), specimen catalogue (where/when), all
  cross-referenced by date and locality. Notes record weather, vegetation,
  vocalisations and other evidence of presence; Grinnell: "Do record them all!"
  and "You can't tell in advance which observations will prove valuable"
  (P: MVZ page; P: Donna Long on the journal). Tracks and signs, and how they were
  found, belong in the journal (P).
- Uncertainty is a formal field. iNaturalist grades certainty (certain, likely,
  uncertain, not identifiable); recorders use "sp." (genus only) and "cf." (resembles
  but differs) (S). Grinnell and Storer define "field characters" as the features
  by which an animal is recognised outdoors (S).
- Layout: write on one side, leaving the facing side for sketches, maps and photos
  (P: Donna Long).
- Not confirmed from opened pages: measurements and sketch conventions (the MVZ
  page does not mention them; the Smithsonian bird field-book listing that mentions
  measurements is S only).

Rules:
1. Each entry carries date, place, weather, habitat, and how the animal was found
   (sign first, animal second).
2. Separate seen from inferred, and grade the identification.
3. Prefer measured things (tracks, stride, droppings, size against a known object)
   to adjectives.
4. Terse notes and a loose sketch are the form; a stated gap ("not observed feeding")
   is both realism and the goosebumps.
5. Leave questions open on the page; the humour comes from understatement.
