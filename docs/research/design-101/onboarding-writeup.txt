# Onboarding and teaching mechanics

Scope: how games teach without (or with little) tutorial, and what research says about load. Sources are in
`onboarding-notes.jsonl`, one row each with the URL and whether it was read in full or only seen as a snippet.
Evidence labels: **developer**, **research**, **verbatim**, **practitioner**, **opinion**, **snippet**.
Builds on, and does not repeat, `teach-automation.md` (Factorio's redesign, red dots, automation rules confusion).
Searched about 14 queries and fetched about 12 pages; several of the best primary sources (Thorson's, Hempuli's,
Blow's and Subset's talks) were unreachable or paywalled, so those games are covered by commentary only and flagged.

# Part A. General

## A1. Whether a tutorial pays depends on how discoverable the mechanics are

**A tutorial earned its keep only in the complex game of three tested; in the two simpler ones it did nothing measurable.**
- Andersen et al., CHI 2012: eight tutorial designs across three games, 45,000+ players. Tutorials raised play time by
  up to 29% (progress up to 75%) in the most complex game (Foldit) and "did not significantly improve player
  engagement" in the two simpler ones. Authors: tutorial investment "may not be justified for games with mechanics
  that can be discovered through experimentation" (research, full PDF read). The four varied dimensions were tutorial presence,
  context-sensitivity, freedom and on-demand help. Instructions given at the moment of need rather than in an up-front
  manual raised play time 16% and progress 40% in Foldit and did nothing in the other two. Restricting player freedom to
  focus attention showed no benefit. On-demand help helped Foldit, did nothing in Hello Worlds and had a negative
  effect in Refraction. Authors: players of experimentable games "seem to learn more from experimentation than from
  reading text".
- Game Developer's survey of tutorial types, built on the same paper's four dimensions (presence, context sensitivity,
  freedom, availability of help), adds: an active-learning tutorial fails if the player has no isolated place to
  practise a complex skill; no single method fits all games (practitioner, read in full).
- Implication: decide per mechanic, not per game. Simple mechanics get no teaching; the one hard one gets a
  practice space.

## A2. The level is the lesson when the order of encounters does the teaching

**Classic no-tutorial teaching is an ordering discipline: safe first exposure, then a test, then a twist.**
- Super Mario Bros. 1-1: the forced first mushroom exists, per the designers, because "we wanted to figure out a way for
  players to be really satisfied with the bigger Mario. And so that's actually why we created this small Mario first"
  (developer, quoted in BGR, read in full). The Iwata Asks page (New Super Mario Bros. Wii, vol. 1 part 4, read in
  full) has programmer Nakago saying levels were not built from screen one: World 3 or 5 came first, beginners found
  them incredibly difficult, and "our basic principle is to move easier features to the beginning of the level".
  1-1 was built to guarantee the player gets the mushroom (the first pipe is placed because the mushroom bounces off
  it), simulated on paper with fallback paths, made near the end and adjusted to the last minute; Iwata notes players
  did not read the manual (developer). The earlier claim that Miyamoto said the forced mushroom shows how it differs
  from a Goomba is not on that page (unverified).
- Commentary on 1-1 (slow first Goomba, pits only after the means to cross them) is a reader's inference, not
  Miyamoto's words (opinion, snippet).
- Portal: Valve's own commentary calls the game "effectively an extended player training exercise" (Walker). The
  method is "checklisting": break a new mechanic into the core components players must grasp, give "a very clean room
  to safely experiment with it", and only then "take a previously learned rule and turn it on its head" (developer,
  Game Informer read in full; commentary transcript read in full).
- The Witness reviews describe teach, check, then expand and invert; Blow's own statements were not found (opinion,
  snippet). Celeste and Hollow Knight: critics say they introduce one twist per chapter or teach by level design; no
  designer statement was found for either, on a second search too (Celeste's level-design GDC page is members-only; Team Cherry material found covers area design, not teaching) (opinion, snippet; null).

## A3. Playtests find the same teaching failures again and again

**The recurring failures are wrong assumed models, missed cues, and too many new ideas in one step.**
- Valve, from playtests: players assumed portals led to other dimensions (fixed by a memorable start room), assumed
  orange portals were exit-only (puzzle added to correct it), "charge[d] down the stairs without noticing what was
  creating the portals", and one puzzle "introduced too many new concepts at once". Jeep Barnett: "without some serious
  prompting, players will rarely look up." (developer, commentary transcript, read in full).
- The method is observe-and-patch: when testers miss a required element, make it more noticeable or change the
  approach (developer, Game Informer).
- Baba Is You players complain when a teaching level comes after the need for it, and when a solution needs an
  interaction not yet shown (verbatim, Steam, snippets). Order errors are the visible complaint, not missing text.

## A4. Players do not read; they want to touch

**Text-heavy instruction is skipped; the measured numbers are informal.**
- One author observed more than 80% of players clicking straight through the instruction screen of a demo at events
  (practitioner, snippet; page 403).
- A mobile developer reported from logs that instructions were barely read and that drop-out came from being made to wait
  before touching controls (practitioner, snippet; article body unreachable).
- One postmortem reports tutorial completion rising from 55% to 85% and early churn down 20% after splitting it into
  chunks with a goal and an immediate reward each, plus tooltips triggered by action or inaction. No sample size or
  control group, game unnamed; treat as an anecdote, not a rate (practitioner, read in full).
- No controlled study of popup dismissal was found; a null, not a negative.

## A5. Just in time, with supports that fade

**Teach at the moment of first need, and thin the support as the player shows they have it.**
- Braid reveals its rewind help after the first death, "when it is first necessary", while Hearts of Iron III teaches
  everything before play; the survey sees merit in each for different audiences (practitioner, read in full).
- Learning research: guidance that helps novices becomes redundant, and can hurt, for people who already know the
  material (expertise reversal, Kalyuga); unguided search overloads novices' working memory; the remedy proposed is
  to fade worked examples as expertise grows. One study of less-structured tasks found no reversal (research,
  Kalyuga 2007 review read in full; no game study was found). The review's conclusion: the effect replicated across
  many materials and 2,200+ students, as a full reversal or more often a partial one, with guidance to be provided at
  the appropriate time and unnecessary support removed as learners progress; its limits are few studies of optimal
  methods and mostly well-defined technical domains. So both unguided discovery and fixed full guidance have
  documented costs, and the middle is guidance that shrinks.
- Segmenting lessons into smaller pieces has a published effect in multimedia learning, but one software-tutorial study
  found no gain in performance and another found fleeting transfer on delayed tests (research, snippets). Chunking
  is a reasonable default, not a proven win.
- Idle genre note: a developer of an idle title worried that 15 minutes shows the player a year of loop, so the game
  must show what comes later (developer, snippet).

# Part B. What it means for Ostmere

Read: `decisions.md` ("Fun, but never vying for attention", "Automation is designed, not clicked"),
`design/opening-screen.md` ("Lessons", "Dots and the one glow", "One thing at a time", "A feature's card").

## B1. Agreements

**The current design already follows the strongest findings: just-in-time, one system at a time, on the real controls, skippable.**
- "Lessons": a system is taught on its real controls "the moment it first matters, and only then", never two at once,
  with `Skip` at every step and nothing modal. This is Valve's practice area made of the live UI, and Braid's
  teach-at-first-need (A5, A2).
- "Lessons": the rule lesson is taught in terms of the mix, a previously learned rule extended. That matches "turn a
  learned rule on its head" (A2).
- "Fun, but never vying for attention", chip arrives "with its answer": the first status meets the player with its cure
  already on screen, which is Valve's clean-room-first ordering for a problem (A2).
- Simple things (first cards, taps) get no lesson, only the one glow. Consistent with A1.
- The one-at-a-time arrival queue and a tap that skips to the next line answer A4: input is never held.

## B2. Gaps and conflicts

**The lessons are the full-guidance kind and never fade, and nothing reports where they fail.**
- Both lessons are step-by-step instruction at the same density. The rule lesson re-teaches the editor's pattern (pick,
  save, find the row) that the mix lesson just taught. A5 says guidance should thin; the second lesson is the natural
  place and does not.
- Several steps are "(read)" steps that end on a reading timer, with a line on the control's meaning. A4 says lines
  are skimmed. Those steps teach the least and could be moved to the card or dropped where the screen already shows
  the result.
- "Done or skipped, a lesson is kept ... and never returns". A skip at step 1 and a completed lesson are treated the
  same. Valve learns from where testers drop; here the drop point is not recorded in the findings I could see, so the
  too-many-concepts failure (A3) could be present and invisible. I have not read the telemetry code; this may exist.
- "Skip is available" and "never returns" together leave a player who skipped by reflex (A4) with no path back, which
  is Andersen's "availability of help" dimension left at zero after the first encounter. The galaxy review's
  91 hand-taps (`galaxy-review.md`) is a related miss: a lesson existed to prevent it only if the player took it.

## B3. Proposals to test

1. **Fade the rule lesson.** If the mix lesson was completed, the rule lesson drops the steps whose controls repeat the
   editor's shape (the save step and the "where it lives" read) and keeps only what is new: the when/do pair. Test:
   completion and Skip rates for the rule lesson, faded vs full, and whether players who saw the faded version
   still build a working rule. This treats A5's fade as a tweak on the existing lesson data (`game/lessons.ts`), not a
   new system.
2. **Record the step each Skip lands on, and the step where a lesson waits longest.** One count per lesson step in the
   existing decision log. If Skip clusters on one step or a "(read)" step, that is the Valve "too many ideas" or
   "nobody reads it" signal (A3, A4) and the step is cut or converted to a visible result. The cost is a log field
   and no UI; it also makes later fade decisions evidence-based.
3. **Keep the lesson's offer reachable once, quietly, after a skip.** A skipped lesson (not a finished one) leaves the
   original quiet dot on its trigger's Add entry or rules tab until the player does the thing it taught, rather than
   vanishing. This reuses the dot's existing meaning ("one of the new things, not yet at work") and does not add a
   modal, a glow or a counter, so it keeps the calm rule in "Fun, but never vying". Test: does it reduce the
   hand-tap pattern the review reported without any complaint of nagging. If the dot is already doing this, the
   proposal is moot and the finding is simply that a skip does not erase the offer.

Not proposed: a pre-game tutorial, an upfront how-to-play screen, a glow on the lesson trigger beyond the existing
rules. A1 and A4 argue against all three for a game this discoverable.
