# Playtesting and reading players

Scope of the evidence: about ten searches and ten fetches. Primary sources on playtest method are thin and partly paywalled
(Hodent's book and the Microsoft Games User Research chapters were not readable; Valve's 2009 slides and the RITE paper
were read in full in a second pass); much of what circulates is unsourced practitioner advice repeating itself. Findings below say what was
actually seen. Source list: `playtesting-notes.jsonl`.

# Part A. General

**1. Watching what a tester does is the signal; what they say afterwards is a lead to be checked against it.**
- developer: Valve's 2009 playtesting slides list direct observation as the core method, with "importance of what people
  do, not what they say" as its plus and "presence of observers can bias results" and "behavior requires interpretation" as
  its minuses; Q&A is marked "people don't know why they do what they do"; think-aloud is good for "why" questions
  but "interferes with gameplay" and is "inaccurate and biased" (Ambinder, GDC 2009 slides, read in full).
- practitioner: guides converge on staying silent, logging hesitations and stuck points, and not explaining mid-session,
  because an explanation gives the tester information the shipped game will not (bugnet.io guides; snippet-level).
- practitioner: testers cannot always say why they feel something and propose fixes from a flawed model of the design,
  so take the problem they report and discard the remedy (same guides; "problems yes, solutions no" is the common
  phrasing, but no primary source for it was found).
- practitioner: Bromley's methods page calls think-aloud "an unnatural task" with cognitive load that "may lead people to the
  right answer", best in co-op games where players explain to each other; a moderator should stay neutral but not sit
  silent (gamesuserresearch.com, read in full). RITE sessions themselves used think-aloud (Medlock et al.).
- developer: Valve's Faliszek says the team first blamed outside testers and by the third or fourth round accepted "it's our
  fault" (Game Developer; read in full).
- developer: Valve's own slide names "lots of biases in current playtesting procedures" as the reason to add
  physiological measures, and pairs them with survey answers rather than replacing them (Ambinder, GDC 2011 slides).
- opinion: the observer effect is real (testers act bolder or more careful when watched); keep the observer's presence
  small, or record and watch later.

**2. "Five users" is a rule about iterating, not about sufficiency, and it is weaker than its reputation.**
- research: Nielsen's model is problems found = N(1-(1-L)^n) with L about 31%; his stated conditions are that the
  five are followed by a fix and a fresh five, that users are comparable, and that each distinct user group gets 3-4
  (nngroup.com).
- research: critics found far less on the web: the first five caught about 35% of a larger set's problems (Spool &
  Schroeder; unverified: the CHI abstract's own text was not retrievable, the abstract via snippet says 49 users on four
  sites while a secondary page says 18 users, so the figures are second-hand), and testers 6-18 each still found 5+ new ones (Perfetti & Landesman). Virzi's own spread was 55% to
  nearly 100% for a five-person sample (humanfactors.com newsletter; snippet; web sites, not games).
- research: RITE (Medlock et al., Microsoft, UPA 2002, primary paper read in full) turns this into a loop: fix the moment
  one tester exposes a problem and the fix is clear, then keep testing. AoE II tutorial: 16 testers, none had played a
  real-time strategy game; changes between testers 1, 2, 5, 8, 9 and 10, then 6 more with no regression. After those 6
  the authors put it at 88% likely that every fixed problem was fixed if it hit 30% of users, 47% if it hit 10%.
  31 problems found, 30 fixed; 6 of 36 fixes had to be redone (one wording fix failed on the next tester). Unclear-cause
  issues get more testers before any fix. Preconditions the paper states: must-pass tasks agreed before the test, fixes in
  under 2 hours or by the next test day, decision makers present, an experienced researcher. A stopping rule is the
  part most teams drop. After 1 change-free tester you have only ruled out problems that hit
  80% or more of users, after 6 about a quarter, after 10 about 15% (measuringu.com, citing the RITE authors). (unverified: no study
  comparing RITE against ordinary rapid testing was found; absence only from search.) It needs fixes that take hours, not days.
- verdict for a small team: few testers, many rounds, a fix between each, and "no one stumbled on the last N" as the
  stop. A single tester proves little; one session's accident (a tester who misread once) is not a finding until a
  second tester repeats it or you can see the cause.

**3. Usability testing and fun testing are different studies with different sample sizes; do not run them as one.**
- practitioner: usability asks "could they do it, where did they stall" and works with a handful watched closely; appreciation
  asks "did they like it" and needs enough players to count, via surveys and longitudinal diaries. Mixing the aims in
  one session gets muddled (PlaytestCloud methods page).
- opinion/practitioner: because games intend challenge, task speed is not a pass criterion; a stall that the player
  enjoys is not a defect.

**4. For games played in short sittings across days, the session shape of the test must match the play.**
- developer: Kolibri tested Idle Miner Tycoon with 7 remote players over three 15-minute sessions separated by two hours,
  to match idle play. It exposed a stall that a single sitting would not: players poured upgrades into one part
  and ignored the part that moved resources, so progress slowed. Contextual teaching at that point fixed it (vendor
  case study; effect sizes not given).
- verdict: for idle and incremental games one long observed session answers the opening; it cannot answer the
  return visit. A test with gaps is a different, cheaper instrument than a live session.

**5. Telemetry says where people leave; only a watched session says why, so use each for what it can do.**
- practitioner: funnels and time-to-first-churn locate exits; analysts pair them with qualitative sessions
  (adriancrook.com, vgm.co; snippet).
- research (weak): deltaDNA's 275 games had 31% D1 retention when the first session exceeded the 9-minute mean against 20%
  below; the author concedes short sessions may be the result of churn, not its cause (Game Developer 2016). Do
  not set a session-length target from it.
- practitioner: benchmark retention figures for mobile are vendor-reported and conflict (D1 21-27%); no idle-specific
  drop-off source was found. A single-player game with a handful of players has no usable funnel statistics; its
  telemetry is a log read as a story (what did this one person tap, in what order, when did they return).

**6. The developer is a poor judge of whether the game is clear, and a genre-literate developer is worst.**
- developer: Hodent names it the "curse of knowledge": "You know your design, so it's really hard to have objective
  knowledge of it, because you can't step back"; her remedy is to bring in the target audience and watch them (DigiPen
  talk report; secondhand, not her own text).
- opinion: once you know something you cannot imagine not knowing it; designers see where objectives are and what the
  intended strategy is (student reflection, wayline.io; no primary study found).
- research: the RITE tutorial test recruited only players with no experience of the genre, because that was the tutorial's
  audience (Medlock et al.).
- practitioner: the common remedy is to test with players new to the genre alongside fans, since the contrast shows
  both the strengths and the gaps. A genre-literate tester supplies motive and vocabulary the newcomer lacks and so
  hides exactly the gaps the first minutes must close.
- verdict: treat the developer's own play as a smoke test (does it run, is the pacing plausible), never as a clarity test.

**7. A survey is a weak instrument; keep it short, piloted, and about the one thing the session did not show.**
- practitioner: completion falls after about five minutes and past ten only a self-selected subset finish; one idea per
  question; include a "never" option; pilot it as a qualitative test; end with a broad open question because
  players use different words from the team (Games User Research expert tips).
- opinion: after a watched session, a recognition check ("which of these lines did you see?") with invented lines
  mixed in measures what was seen without asking anyone to remember or flatter. Not found in a source; it is standard
  signal-detection practice applied here.

**8. Remote and recorded sessions show behaviour but not the room; the cost is the quiet signals.**
- practitioner: unmoderated and recorded sessions scale and remove the observer effect, but you cannot ask "what were you
  thinking" at the moment, and you lose posture, hesitation off-screen and the glance away (PlaytestCloud methods
  page, bugnet guides; thin evidence). Asking after the fact is, per one developer blog, "vastly inferior" to seeing the
  play (gamedev.net; snippet).

**Gaps.** Not found: Hodent's own text, the Pagulayan et al. chapter (academia 403, game studies host refused), the Spool
& Schroeder full text, a Valve or Portal commentary transcript on "never help the tester" (a search found nothing citing
it), Riot method writing beyond a 2XKO post that names no procedure, Bromley on developers watching sessions (his
pages do not address it), and any solo or small-team multi-session idle-game playtest account. Said plainly rather than filled.

# Part B. What it means for Ostmere

Practice already agrees with Part A on several points; two gaps are worth a test.

**Where practice agrees**
- **Cold tester, not the author.** The queue item on teaching the mix says it is "judged by a cold tester who doesn't play
  idle games, never the owner", and `decisions.md`, "Shown in the design, not claimed" and the opening-screen doc's four
  fixes all come from cold testers' stalls (eyes split, type small, first card lost, front page unclear). That is finding 6 applied
  correctly; the owner being genre-literate says the same.
- **Behaviour over report.** The "was it seen" probe (`app/tools/seen/`) plus the planned "pick the seen lines from
  real and invented ones" step is the recognition check of finding 7. Keep it as the instrument for story-line
  reach rather than asking testers whether they noticed.
- **Return visits.** The queue held day two until day one is shared and testers' feedback is in, and says to
  judge notifications from the returns in the telemetry. That is the right order for finding 4.
- **Fix, then retest.** Small lines ship unreviewed and the game is developed live (`decisions.md`, "Developed live,
  forward-only by preference"); fast fixes are the condition RITE needs.

**Gaps and conflicts**
- **No stopping rule.** Testers arrive as "one tester session per change" with no statement of when a lesson is
  done. Without a stop, either one tester's accident is treated as a finding or a lesson is never called finished.
- **No gap session.** Every probe described is a single sitting (the simulated player, the seen probe, the cold tester
  watched live). The game's own design is short visits across days ("Something to watch, any time, for hours", the pacing
  docs), and the new mix and rule lessons teach on a trigger from repeated behaviour (the strip offering a card after
  repeated hand taps). Whether that trigger fires on a person returning after hours is untested by anyone except the owner.
- **Telemetry is read as a count, not a story.** With a few testers, the decision log is best read per person in order.

**Proposals to test**
1. **A RITE-style stop for the mix and rule lessons.** Cold testers one at a time; fix what a tester stalled on before the
   next; stop after three consecutive testers who reach the first built mix and first built rule without help, and
   report it as "no problem hitting more than about half of testers found" rather than "verified". Cheap because
   lessons are data (issue #22, "Teach the mix system where it is first needed"). Pass condition stated before the
   first session, and the observer says nothing.
2. **A gapped cold-tester pair.** Two or three cold testers, each playing two short sittings a few hours apart (the
   Kolibri shape), with the second sitting opened from the return screen. Read from the decision log and screen
   recording: do the repeated-tap and repeated-switch offers appear on the return, are the offers taken, and what
   does the tester do first. Answers the day-two question without waiting for the public build.
3. **A per-person decision-log read after each tester,** as a short fixed table (time to first tap, first idle gap
   over a minute and what the screen showed then, offers shown versus taken, last action before leaving), written
   by the session before any fix. The point is the drop-off point of one person, not an average. Pair each leave
   point with a one-line question to that tester afterwards ("what were you about to do?"), not a rating.

Do not use survey ratings to decide lesson changes at this sample size; use them only for the open "anything else" question.
